The theory under test: mosquitoes and ticks might carry the same viruses, in the same indigenous areas — so the research needs to be looked upon. This page is that look: which viruses are actually documented in both bug groups in nature, where, and how strong the evidence is. Research read 3 November 2026; a general analysis, not medical advice.

Same bugs' viruses? Almost never — and never proven end‑to‑end.

Mosquitoes and ticks overlap on the map and in the season, but the major viruses they carry are almost all single-vector-group viruses. A small number of viruses have been detected in both groups in nature — yet no major human pathogen was found with an established, sustained cycle that runs through both a mosquito and a tick in the same place and time.

The verdict on the theory

Overlap of bugs: yes. Overlap of viruses: mostly no. Where both bugs live in the same area — the Cascades, the Northeast, the Great Lakes — they carry different viruses. A person can catch a mosquito virus and a tick virus in the same season, from two different bites. That is co-exposure, not a shared virus.

The strongest “shared” cases are all edge cases: viruses found in both groups where transmission by one group is unproven, historical isolations from the 1970s with no modern cycle shown, or laboratory results that never became a cycle in nature. Each one is graded below, never upgraded.

Virus

The virus species itself. A name in this column says nothing yet about what carries it.

Vector

The mosquito or tick species. Detection in a tick pool is not proof the tick transmits — an engorged tick may just hold a recent blood meal.

Disease

The human illness. Antibodies in a patient show exposure — not which bug delivered the virus.

Test

PCR or isolation in a bug pool, serology in a host, a clinical test in a patient — three different kinds of evidence, never interchangeable.

What “shared” would actually have to prove

Before the table: the bar. A virus earns “established dual-vector cycle” only by clearing all three rungs. Every dual-detection case in the research stalls on rung 1, 2, or 3 — and the table below says exactly where.

RUNG 1 — FIELD

Found in both, in the same focus

Repeated field isolation of the same virus species from both mosquitoes and ticks in the same place.

Jingmenviruses clear this rung — and stop here.
RUNG 2 — LABORATORY

Each bug can actually pass it on

Each vector group acquires the virus by feeding, it replicates and reaches the salivary glands, and the bug transmits it by bite to a susceptible host.

Sindbis virus clears this rung in ticks — in the lab only.
RUNG 3 — NATURE

The cycle sustains itself outdoors

Evidence the two-vector cycle is maintained in nature, not just possible under glass.

No virus in the research clears all three rungs.

A review cited in the vesicular stomatitis literature makes the same general point: individual arboviruses are transmitted by a single type of vector — the one named exception, vesicular stomatitis virus, is carried by sandflies and mosquitoes, not ticks, so it does not join the mosquito–tick list (see Gap 7).

In plain wordsScientists have occasionally found the same virus inside both a mosquito and a tick. But finding a virus inside a bug is like finding a letter inside a mailbox — it doesn't prove that mailbox delivers. Delivery — bite transmission — is what's missing in every case below.

Same indigenous areas? The bugs overlap — the viruses don't

Region by region, with the mosquito species, tick species, and virus named, and whether detection was ever in the same place and time. Two regions get a flat “no.” Two get “same region, transmission unproven.” One study is a clean negative control.

NO shared virus documented

Cascade Range / Pacific Northwest

Vectors overlap; viruses do not, on the sources found. The mosquito side and the tick side of the same states carry entirely different virus lists:

Mosquito side

  • West Nile virus — Culex pipiens, Cx. tarsalis; Jackson County, Oregon tests Culex pools for WNV/WEE by RT-PCR.
  • Western equine encephalitis virus — Culex tarsalis; prevalent in eastern Washington (Yakima Valley).
  • St. Louis encephalitis virus — Culex tarsalis in the western US.
  • Jamestown Canyon / snowshoe hare viruses — California-serogroup mosquitoes occur in western Canada / PNW-adjacent areas; Culiseta inornata is a northern vector.

Tick side

  • Colorado tick fever virus — Dermacentor andersoni (Rocky Mountain wood tick), across the western US including Washington, Oregon and Idaho at ~4,000–10,000 ft; hosts are ground squirrels, marmots and chipmunks.
  • West Nile in ticks? Isolations exist elsewhere (Kenya, Europe) — no PNW tick cycle was found.
  • Powassan? Washington DOH lists it for reporting, but its recent-trends note names no locally acquired Powassan case; other than WNV, the last locally acquired arboviral infection in WA was St. Louis encephalitis in 2023, before that Western equine encephalitis in 1988.
Gap, stated plainly: Ixodes pacificus is the PNW Ixodes species, but the sources name it for Lyme, anaplasmosis and relapsing fever (bacterial) — not for Powassan virus. Do not transfer the Northeast I. scapularis–Powassan association to I. pacificus. And no Cascade-specific (as opposed to state-level) vector–virus survey was found at all, so a claim that “the Cascades have a shared mosquito–tick virus” is unsupported.
NO shared virus — co-exposure only

US Northeast / Great Lakes

Both vector groups are indigenous, both virus groups concentrate in the same states, and both peak bimodally in spring and late summer. Minnesota starts testing Powassan on 1 April (tick activity above 40 °F) and Jamestown Canyon on 1 April (post-snowmelt mosquitoes) — so one person can be exposed to both in a single season. That is the whole story; it is not a shared virus.

Mosquito-only here

  • Jamestown Canyon virus — Aedes, Culiseta, Coquillettidia spp. (26+ species reported capable); white-tailed deer amplify it.
  • West Nile virus — Culex pipiens / Cx. restuans.
  • Eastern equine encephalitis virus — Culiseta melanura (enzootic), bridge vectors Coquillettidia / Aedes.
  • La Crosse virus — Aedes triseriatus.

Tick-only here

  • Powassan virus — Ixodes scapularis (lineage 2, Northeast); I. cookei / I. marxi (lineage 1, Great Lakes).

What the case reports actually prove: a 2023 case describes Lyme disease (tick, Borrelia burgdorferi) plus Jamestown Canyon (mosquito) in one patient — “contracted … through a tick bite and … through a mosquito bite,” enabled by “overlapping geographical areas of these two vectors.” A 2025 case had Powassan encephalitis plus Jamestown Canyon seropositivity as a diagnostic dilemma. Neither case shows one virus in both vectors.

Same region, probable same milieu — transmission unproven

Ijara District, north-eastern Kenya — the strongest same-focus field evidence

A pastoralist / wildlife interface where 10,488 ticks from livestock and wildlife were pooled (1,520 pools; 76% Rhipicephalus pulchellus, plus Hyalomma truncatum, Amblyomma gemma, A. lepidum).

Tick isolates

  • Bunyamwera 44 · Semliki Forest 25 · West Nile 3 · Ndumu 2 — all mosquito-borne viruses found inside ticks.
  • Thogoto 3 and Dugbe 5 — tick-borne, as expected.

Why it still isn't proof

  • Mosquito strains of Semliki Forest virus from the same North Eastern Province cluster closely with the tick isolate, and WNV had previously been isolated from Kenyan mosquitoes — closely related strains, same province.
  • But the study did not sample mosquitoes and ticks simultaneously at one site and date, and did not demonstrate tick-to-host transmission.
  • The likely explanation is shared hosts: warthogs, cattle, sheep and goats in one pastoralist system are bitten by both mosquitoes and several tick species — maximising cross-detection, blood meals included.
Same virus, different studies — never one focus, one time, with transmission

Cameroon · China · Kyrgyzstan / Volga

Yaoundé / Garoua, Cameroon (Jingmenvirus): mosquito sampling in 2022–2023 across rainy and dry seasons and two biotopes found human-derived Jingmenvirus in 4 mosquito species, and the near-identical human plasma virus came from the same area. But tick sampling for that same strain in the same area and time was not reported — the tick link is imported from the wider Jingmenvirus literature (China, Brazil, etc.). Same area for mosquito + human; no Cameroon tick–mosquito pair.

China (ALSV / JMTV) and Russia / Finland / France / Germany (ALSV): tick detections (Ixodes persulcatus, I. ricinus) and mosquito detections (Anopheles yatsushiroensis, Aedes vexans, Culex pipiens pallens, Cx. tritaeniorhynchus) are reported for the same virus species — in different studies, years and regions. No single study in the sources found reports both vector groups from one focus at one time with a transmission demonstration.

Kyrgyzstan (Batken) / Volga estuary, Russia (Dhori): historical isolations from both ticks and mosquitoes in the same broad region, from the 1970s, without modern follow-up in the sources found.

Clean negative control — strictly segregated

Senegal (Matam + Thiès), Sep 2022 – Mar 2024

One integrated study sampled 6,558 mosquitoes (23 species) and 1,904 ticks (7 species) over two seasons, in overlapping geography, at the same time — exactly the design that could catch a shared virus if one were there.

In mosquitoes only

  • Rift Valley fever virus — found exclusively in Aedes vexans mosquito pools (Matam).

In ticks only

  • Crimean–Congo haemorrhagic fever virus — in multiple tick species, both regions, both seasons; Hyalomma rufipes predominant in the dry season.

Same study, same seasons, overlapping geography — different viruses, strictly segregated by vector group. Overlap alone does not mix the virus lists.

The pattern underneath: within a single genus, vector group is usually conserved. Orthoflavivirus splits into a mosquito-borne branch (West Nile, St. Louis, dengue, yellow fever, Japanese encephalitis) and a tick-borne branch (TBEV, Powassan, louping ill, Kyasanur) — roughly 50% of flaviviruses are mosquito-borne and ~28% tick-borne (ICTV 9th Report). The Jingmenviruses are the exception that proves the pattern is ecological, not a law of taxonomy.

What lets a virus use both bugs — and what blocks it

Six mechanisms were checked against the sources, including the size question. Five shape the answer. One — virus size — does not, and the chart below shows why.

1 · Vector competence is a chain of barriers

Competence means: acquire the virus in a blood meal → infect the midgut → escape the midgut → spread through the body → infect the salivary glands → leave in saliva at the next bite. Fail at any barrier and the bug is incompetent — even if the virus is detectable inside it. That single fact explains most of the table above.

Midgut infection barrier→Midgut escape barrier→Salivary gland barrier

Ticks don't map neatly onto the mosquito model: their salivary glands are grape-like branched acini (mosquito glands are lobed), and one review raises the possibility that tick-borne viruses pass from body cavity to saliva without infecting the glands at all.

2 · Entry receptors: permissive, not decisive

Alphaviruses (Sindbis, Semliki Forest, the equine viruses) use LDL-receptor family members and their invertebrate counterparts in both mammal and mosquito cells — a key that fits locks on both sides of the divide. Flaviviruses use a broad kit of attachment molecules (C-type lectins, TIM/TAM receptors, integrins, claudin-1, and others).

But no source found identifies a tick-specific receptor whose absence blocks mosquito viruses, or the reverse. Receptors are permissive background; the discriminating steps are downstream — midgut escape, spread, salivary glands — and ecological.

3 · Temperature and time

Higher temperature generally shortens the virus's incubation period inside the bug and raises transmission efficiency; fluctuating natural temperatures change competence. A virus whose incubation outlasts the vector's life or feeding interval cannot be transmitted by that vector, however well its cells grow in a dish.

The two bugs run on different clocks: mosquitoes live weeks and feed in minutes, needing fast spread; ticks feed for days and live months to years, and can carry virus across moults and into their eggs — which is how a tick-persistent virus like West Nile in Ornithodoros can look like a reservoir without being an efficient vector.

4 · Host range and the viraemia threshold

A bug can only pick up a virus from a host that is viraemic enough, for long enough, and that the bug actually bites: West Nile needs viraemic passerine birds for Culex; Jamestown Canyon needs white-tailed deer; Powassan needs small mammals and woodchucks; Colorado tick fever needs ground squirrels, marmots and chipmunks.

A narrow host range that one bug group rarely bites starves the other group of chances — whatever its cells could do. The Kenya pattern is the mirror image: one shared pool of warthogs, cattle, sheep and goats, bitten by everything. Co-feeding transmission (uninfected bugs picking up virus beside infected ones, shown for Thogoto virus in ticks) can bypass viraemia — but it has not been shown to build a mosquito–tick bridge for any virus in the sources.

5 · Innate immunity: a dimmer, not the switch

  • RNA interference is described as the most important antiviral response in mosquitoes and “likely” in all arthropods, acting as a midgut gatekeeper — but for ticks, reviews state plainly: “we know little about the role of RNAi in ticks.”
  • JAK/STAT, Toll and Imd pathways are implicated in insect antiviral defence; in Sindbis-infected ticks, early autophagy then rising cell death were observed.
  • Microbiota: Wolbachia in mosquitoes competes for the cholesterol enveloped viruses need; tick microbiomes are less characterised for this.

Net effect: immunity tunes efficiency within a pairing that already works. Co-evolution matters more — a virus adapted to one vector's immunity may be cleared by, or may kill, the other.

6 · Does virus size play a role? Tested — no

Virion sizes overlap completely across the mosquito/tick divide, so diameter cannot be what sorts viruses into one camp or the other:

The one size-related barrier in the sources — the mosquito midgut's basal lamina, whose pores are smaller than a virus particle — applies to all arboviruses in mosquitoes alike. And the “body size” literature is about the bug's size, not the virus's. Genome organisation doesn't discriminate either: dual-detected, mosquito-only and tick-only viruses all include both segmented and non-segmented genomes.

Conclusion: size is not a discriminating mechanism on current evidence.

Where the evidence is thin — all eleven gaps, marked

Nothing in this section is smoothed over. If a claim you have heard isn't supported, it is here, named.

GAP 1

No established dual cycle — for any major human virus

Every dual-detection case downgrades to detection-only, historical-only, or laboratory-only on inspection.

GAP 2

Cascades / PNW shared virus

No source documents the same virus in both a mosquito and a tick in the Cascades. Ixodes pacificus–Powassan in the PNW is unverified — never infer it from I. scapularis–Powassan in the Northeast.

GAP 3

Northeast / Great Lakes shared virus

Powassan and Jamestown Canyon co-occur regionally and seasonally and can co-infect a person via two bites — but no source shows Jamestown Canyon in a tick, or Powassan in a mosquito, as a natural cycle.

GAP 4

Jingmenvirus mosquito transmission: unproven

Sequence detection in mosquitoes plus a near-identical human case nearby is suggestive, not proof. No mosquito acquisition → spread → bite-transmission study was found.

GAP 5

Kenya tick isolates: transmission unproven

Isolation from engorged ticks on viraemic hosts cannot distinguish a blood-meal passenger from a replicating, transmissible infection. The authors themselves say “potential role” / “remains unknown.”

GAP 6

Thogoto virus mosquito isolate: unresolved

Attributed to “one subtype” without species-level resolution. Do not cite Thogoto virus as dual-vector without that qualifier.

GAP 7

Vesicular stomatitis virus is NOT a mosquito–tick case

It is the cited exception to single-vector transmission — for sandflies + mosquitoes (and blackflies/midges, partly mechanical). No biological tick transmission was verified. Do not add it to the shared list.

GAP 8

TBEV in mosquitoes: historical only

Sporadic isolation from mosquitoes, flies, fleas and lice is noted, with the transmission role “not demonstrated.” A 1950 experimental study does not change the classification.

GAP 9

Serology is not vector proof

Antibodies in a host (Jingmenvirus seroprevalence in China/France; Thogoto/Dhori antibodies in livestock and humans) show exposure, not which arthropod delivered the virus. A positive clinical test in a patient does not identify the vector either.

GAP 10

Surveillance asymmetry

Mosquito pools are routinely tested for WNV/EEE/SLEV/JCV (New Jersey tested 7,099 pools in 2026); tick testing is usually targeted and rarely includes mosquito viruses — and vice versa. So “not detected in the other bug” is weaker evidence than it looks. The experimental incompetence results, though, stand independent of that asymmetry.

GAP 11

Taxonomy drift

California serogroup viruses were Bunyaviridae in older sources, Peribunyaviridae now; Colorado tick fever virus was Reoviridae, now Spinareoviridae. Family names in older papers should be translated, not quoted as conflicts.

ALSO OPEN

The research question itself stays open

The honest summary is asymmetrical: the negative (no proven dual cycle) is well supported by experiments; the positives (Jingmenviruses especially) are recent and partly sequence-only. “Not proven” is not “disproven” — it is where the next study belongs.

Could not verify

  • Any Cascade-specific (county / elevation-band) mosquito + tick virus survey.
  • Mosquito competence for JMTV/ALSV, Batken, Dhori, Bunyamwera, Semliki Forest or Ndumu viruses — no transmission study found.
  • Tick competence for any California-serogroup virus, EEEV, WEEV, VEEV, SLEV, dengue, Zika or chikungunya — no positive study found (not the same as a published negative for every pairing).
  • A virion-size threshold or mechanism separating mosquito from tick viruses — no source found.
