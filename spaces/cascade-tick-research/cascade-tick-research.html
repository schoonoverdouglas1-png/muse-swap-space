Cascade Tick Research

Which ticks bite people in the Cascade Mountain Range, what they actually carry, how that relates to the equine encephalitis viruses and the standard arbovirus antibody panel (ARBOP) — and a direct test of the idea that a smaller virus might live in the cold-blooded tick while a larger or different one lives in the warm-blooded mammal.

How to read this page — four tracks, kept separate. Tick (the vector) ≠ virus or organism ≠ disease ≠ test. A positive antibody means the immune system reacted at some point; it is not proof the organism is present now. A positive PCR means nucleic acid was detected in that tube; it is not by itself a disease diagnosis. This is a general research analysis — not a medical recommendation for any person, no test order, and no laboratory was contacted. For your situation, testing decisions belong to you first, your clinician second.

The one established Cascade tick virus

Colorado tick fever virus (CTFV), carried by the Rocky Mountain wood tick Dermacentor andersoni (and found in D. occidentalis). It is the textbook tick-plus-mammal virus of this region.

Powassan — real, but not proven Cascade

Powassan virus is a genuine tick-borne flavivirus with a Mayo blood PCR (POWVB), but US human cases concentrate in the Northeast and Great Lakes. No Washington case series was found — do not list it as Cascade-endemic without that qualifier.

Equine viruses are not tick viruses here

Eastern, Western, and Venezuelan equine encephalitis viruses are mosquito-borne alphaviruses. A tick role appears only as a possible overwintering or experimental route in older veterinary summaries — not an established Cascade cycle.

Small-in-bug / large-in-mammal: not supported

Arboviruses replicate in both hosts as the same virus. Size is set by viral family, not by the last host. The Cascade tick virus CTFV (~60–80 nm) is actually larger than the equine alphaviruses (~60–70 nm). Full test in §3.

1  The ticks of the Cascades — and what each carries

Oregon has about 20 hard-tick species, but only four are commonly found on humans there. Washington DOH names Ixodes pacificus (western Washington and the eastern Cascade slopes), Dermacentor similis and D. andersoni (eastern Washington), plus soft ticks. “Indigenous” here means established and native in the WA/OR/CA Cascades — not a tick found once on a travelling dog.

Taxonomy note: the western dog tick is now Dermacentor similis, split from the eastern D. variabilis. Older sources still call western specimens D. variabilis — read them as D. similis. Sources: Oregon State / Oregon Extension tick summary · Washington DOH tick data

2  Tick viruses vs the equine encephalitis viruses — shared, or separate?

Separate cycles, with one shared logic. The three equine encephalitis viruses are alphaviruses (family Togaviridae) carried by mosquitoes, with wild birds as the main reservoir. Horses and humans are generally dead-end hosts for Eastern and Western equine encephalitis (not enough virus in the blood to infect the next mosquito) — but equids are amplifying hosts for Venezuelan equine encephalitis.

Is there a tick link at all for EEE / WEE / VEE?

Only weak and secondary — and it must not be presented as the cycle. Older veterinary summaries list “other vectors or overwintering hosts” for WEEV as possibly including Dermacentor andersoni ticks, the assassin bug Triatoma sanguisuga and the cliff-swallow bug, and note epidemic VEEV “may also be transmitted by ticks,” citing a 1937 paper, “The tick as a vector for the virus disease equine encephalomyelitis.” That is experimental / historical, not an established field cycle in the Cascades, and no Cascade human tick-bite WEEV / EEEV / VEEV transmission series was found.

The established answer: different viruses circulate in ticks versus in the mosquito–bird–mammal equine cycle. They overlap only in that both groups are arboviruses, and both can infect mammals — horses and humans included — as dead-end or amplifying hosts.

Viruses documented in BOTH ticks and mammals — the same virus, both hosts

Colorado tick fever virus (CTFV)

Tick: D. andersoni (principal), also D. occidentalis. Mammal: humans, rodents and other mammals. Disease: Colorado tick fever — biphasic (“saddleback”) fever, leukopenia, thrombocytopenia; rarely meningitis / encephalitis. Genome: double-stranded RNA, 12 segments, non-enveloped Coltivirus. This is the textbook Cascade tick-plus-mammal virus.

Powassan virus (POWV)

Lineages 1 and 2 (lineage 2 = deer tick virus). Tick: Ixodes species — I. cookei / marxi for lineage 1; I. scapularis for lineage 2 in the east. Mammal: groundhogs, skunks, squirrels, white-footed mice, humans (dead-end; neuroinvasive disease in a minority). Cascade qualifier: human disease is northeastern / Great Lakes–concentrated per CDC; no WA/OR/CA Cascade human case series was found. A national tick-plus-mammal virus, not a proven Cascade one.

For contrast: the tick-borne encephalitis virus complex (European / Far Eastern / Siberian TBEV) is a tick-plus-mammal virus in Eurasia — not a US Cascade virus — listed only to prevent conflation with Powassan, its North American flavivirus relative. And the mosquito analogues that share the same dual-host logic with a mosquito vector instead: West Nile, St. Louis encephalitis, La Crosse, and Jamestown Canyon viruses.

Tick-only viruses — and the honest gap

Arthropod-specific viruses are defined as reproducing exclusively in arthropods, with “no defined ability to infect vertebrates” — in the flavivirus literature, examples include cell-fusing agent virus, Palm Creek virus, and Parramatta River virus. Jingmenviruses span a spectrum: some (like Jingmen tick virus) infect both arthropods and vertebrates; others are arthropod-only.

Gap, stated plainly: no named Cascade-specific tick-only virus — one found in Cascade D. andersoni, I. pacificus or O. hermsi and proven unable to infect vertebrates — was identified in this research. Tick virome surveys find many such sequences nationally, but naming one for the Cascades specifically would be speculation. The reverse also holds: flaviviruses include “no-known-vector” viruses with no established arthropod vector, and most mammalian viruses are not arboviruses at all. A virus found in a mammal does not make it tick-borne.

3  Your hypothesis, tested: small virus in the bug, larger virus in the mammal?

The idea, in plain terms: ticks are cold-blooded (their temperature follows the air) and humans are warm-blooded (a steady ~37 °C) — so could a small virus sit in the tick, and a larger or different virus sit in the mammal, with the bite passing one, the other, or both?

Verdict: not supported as a within-bite mechanism. It is supported only in two narrower senses: different organisms can genuinely co-infect and be co-transmitted by one bite, and — over evolutionary time — arthropod-specific viruses may be the ancestors of dual-host arboviruses. Neither is one virus turning into a larger, different virus inside the mammal.

What arboviruses actually do in two hosts

Arboviruses are defined by the dual-host requirement: the same virus must replicate in both the arthropod and the vertebrate. Dengue, for example, grows in Aedes mosquitoes at 25–30 °C and in humans at 37 °C. After a blood meal, the virus must infect the midgut, escape it, spread through the haemolymph, infect the salivary glands, and be shed in saliva — the extrinsic incubation period (EIP) — before the next bite can transmit it. Ticks add routes mosquitoes don't have: transstadial passage (stage to stage as the tick moults), transovarial passage (female → eggs, documented for POWV, Babesia and Rickettsia), and co-feeding transmission between ticks feeding close together on the same host, with no viraemia needed.

Temperature: it changes speed, not identity

  • Within optimal limits, warmer conditions shorten the extrinsic incubation period and increase vector efficiency. A virus taking ~14 days to become transmissible at 25 °C might take ~7 days at 30 °C.
  • The relationship is unimodal — “Goldilocks,” not “hotter = more.” Too cold slows metabolism and replication; too hot harms the vector's survival.
  • In vertebrates, arboviruses must replicate at 37–39 °C. The binding constraint is usually vector fitness, not a hard viral temperature ceiling.
  • Insect-specific viruses are the exception that proves the rule: unlike arboviruses, which can replicate at temperatures up to 42 °C, insect-specific viruses replicate only at ambient temperatures, and one insect-specific bunyavirus's replication was hindered by elevated temperatures where arboviral bunyaviruses were not. But lowering the temperature alone is not sufficient to let an insect-specific virus replicate in mammalian cells — temperature is one host-range barrier among several (entry, innate immunity, post-entry blocks), not a size switch. Source: PMC6458977

Size: set by family, identical in tick and mammal

There is no documented process by which a bite converts a small tick virus into a larger mammal virus, or by which the mammal harbours a systematically larger version of the same virus. What does change between hosts: viral titre (load), tissue tropism, duration and persistence — often a persistent, low-harm infection in the vector versus an acute, sometimes cell-damaging infection in the vertebrate — and immune evasion. Not particle diameter, and not genome identity.

What is documented that could look like the hypothesis

  1. Co-infection / co-transmission. One Ixodes tick can carry B. burgdorferi + A. phagocytophilum + Babesia spp. + B. miyamotoi, and one bite can transmit more than one organism. Those are different organisms with different sizes — not one virus changing size.
  2. Evolutionary origin. Insect-specific viruses and arboviruses are evolutionarily related; arboviruses may have been insect-specific viruses that, through evolution, acquired the ability to expand their host range to vertebrates. A phylogenetic hypothesis over evolutionary time — not a per-bite transformation.
  3. Recombination. WEEV is a recombinant of an EEE-like virus and a Sindbis-like virus — again an ancient evolutionary event, not a tick-to-mammal size change.
  4. Serology confusion. An antibody to one flavivirus or alphavirus can cross-react with a related virus (EEE ↔ WEE; St. Louis ↔ dengue). That can look like “a different virus in the mammal” if antibody results are misread as organism identification — which is exactly why the four tracks on this page are kept separate.

5  How this relates to the arbovirus antibody panel (ARBOP)

ARBOP — Arbovirus Antibody Panel, IgG and IgM, Serum (IFA; serum 0.5 mL, min 0.15 mL; LOINC 49093-8; Rochester). Its four components are not available separately: CAVP — California virus (La Crosse) IgG+IgM; EEEP — Eastern equine; STLP — St. Louis; WEEP — Western equine. Reference: IgG and IgM <1:10 for each. IgM is reliably detected 1–3 weeks after onset, peaks, then declines within 3 months; a single IgG ≥1:10 indicates exposure, and a ≥4-fold rise in paired sera indicates recent infection. Spinal-fluid counterpart: ABOPC. Mayo catalog 83267

Bottom line: ARBOP is a mosquito-encephalitis antibody panel, not a tick panel. Its overlap with the Cascade picture is exactly one historically western virus (WEEV) plus three non-tick viruses included for the national encephalitis differential. A negative ARBOP does not exclude Cascade tick-borne infection, and a positive ARBOP does not identify a tick, a tick-borne organism, or current infection — that takes paired titres, PRNT, exposure history, and clinical correlation.

6  Open questions — what could not be verified

  1. No Mayo blood PCR found for Colorado tick fever virus, Rickettsia rickettsii, R. philipii, or Francisella tularensis (species-specific), or a dedicated Borrelia hermsii PCR. BRBST is the only Mayo blood route that could in principle identify the bacterial three — but it is broad-range and second-tier, not species-specific.
  2. No Cascade-specific tick-only virus was named and verified, and no Cascade human Powassan case series was found (Powassan is northeastern / Great Lakes–concentrated).
  3. Exact POWVS (serum Powassan PCR) ordering details, Mayo Rickettsia-antibody test IDs, and the current CDC Colorado tick fever test-directory status were not individually verified — CDC itself warns some CTF tests are “not currently available at CDC.”
  4. WEEV “last case” year disagrees across sources — 1994 in the genome paper, 1999 in secondary summaries, and WA-specific 1988 per WA DOH. Reported here as a disagreement, not resolved.
  5. Whether ARBOP's La Crosse antigen cross-detects Jamestown Canyon virus antibody at a clinically reportable level — not verified.
  6. Ehrlichia species and B. microti in TIKLB reflect a national panel, not Cascade endemicity; local prevalence of each TIKLB organism in the Cascades specifically (versus the Pacific Northwest generally) was not quantified from a Cascade-only dataset.
  7. Dermacentor similis vs variabilis taxonomy varies by source vintage; older literature cited as D. variabilis for western specimens should be read as D. similis under current taxonomy.
  8. Not every Mayo page received live-browser confirmation: TIKLB, ARBOP, ABOPC, POWVB, the WA relapsing-fever and arboviral pages, and the CDC CTF page were opened; other Mayo pages are index / search results. Test availability can change — re-check the Mayo catalog at time of use.
